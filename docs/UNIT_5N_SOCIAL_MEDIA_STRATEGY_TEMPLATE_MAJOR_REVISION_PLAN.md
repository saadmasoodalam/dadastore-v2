## 5. Reason major revision is required

The current article is useful as general advice, but it does not fully satisfy the promise created by the word “template.”

Its central seven-part sequence closely repeats the existing published article, **How to Create a Social Media Marketing Plan That Drives Results**:

- business objective;
- audience;
- platform role;
- content pillars;
- production;
- measurement;
- review.

That overlap creates two problems:

1. the new article does not have a sufficiently distinct editorial purpose; and
2. readers searching for a template receive mostly narrative guidance rather than a compact structure they can complete and reuse.

Minor trimming would not solve the issue. The core article architecture must change from an explanatory guide into a static operating template with explicit fields, prompts, outputs, ownership, and review instructions.

- the structure overlaps the existing published planning guide;

## 10. Recommended article purpose

The revised article should help a small business create a short social media operating strategy by completing eight decisions:

1. business objective;
2. audience definition;
3. platform selection;
4. content pillars;
5. publishing approach;
6. engagement process;
7. KPI selection;
8. review and adjustment.

The output should be a concise internal document that:

- guides content and channel decisions;
- identifies owners and constraints;
- records what the team will not do;
- separates assumptions from evidence;
- connects activity to a review decision;
- sits above the content calendar.

The article's distinct promise should be:

> Use this page to complete a practical strategy document; use the published planning guide when deeper explanation of the wider planning system is needed.

This purpose distinguishes the revised article from the existing published guide.

## 11. Recommended content architecture

The revised article should use this editorial architecture:

1. **Short diagnostic introduction**  
   Explain why reactive posting is not a strategy and state the template's output.
2. **Strategy versus schedule**  
   Define the difference among strategy, publishing approach, and content calendar.
3. **How to complete the template**  
   Set a review period, gather inputs, record evidence confidence, and assign an owner.
4. **Eight static template blocks**  
   Each block contains:
   - decision to make;
   - fill-in prompts;
   - evidence or inputs;
   - final output field;
   - owner;
   - review trigger.
5. **One completed hypothetical example**  
   Show how the eight outputs connect without presenting a client result.
6. **How to use the completed strategy**  
   Explain planning, publishing, engagement, measurement, and review cadence.
7. **Mistakes and quality check**  
   Focus on template misuse rather than repeating broad social media mistakes.
8. **Final completion checklist**
9. **Public-safe DaDaStore CTA**

The architecture should be visibly more operational and less essay-like than the existing published guide.

## 17. Template usability requirements

Each of the eight template blocks must contain:

- **Decision:** the specific choice being made;
- **Why it matters:** one concise explanation;
- **Write this:** two to five fill-in prompts;
- **Use this evidence:** approved input types or an explicit assumption label;
- **Output:** one concise field or statement the team should record;
- **Owner:** who is responsible;
- **Review trigger/date:** when the choice must be revisited.

The full completed template should fit into a short internal document. It should be possible to copy the field labels into a document without copying all explanatory prose.

Usability rules:

- prompts use plain language;
- fields do not require specialist marketing terminology;
- optional starting ranges are labeled as optional;
- “unknown” and “assumption to test” are valid answers;
- every section supports a decision;
- the content calendar is a downstream artifact, not a replacement for strategy;
- the static template remains embedded in article HTML;
- no download, form submission, local storage, account, generator, or JavaScript is added;
- no new external dependency is introduced.

## 18. Practical examples required

The revised article should include:

### One end-to-end hypothetical example

Use a clearly labeled fictional small business, not a DaDaStore client. The example should fill all eight outputs without reporting results.

Safe example shape:

- a small service or ecommerce business;
- one bounded business objective;
- one priority audience situation;
- one primary platform role and one “not now” decision;
- three illustrative content territories presented as an example, not a universal rule;
- a manageable publishing approach based on stated capacity;
- an engagement owner and escalation path;
- one meaningful action plus diagnostic signals;
- a scheduled review with maintain, adjust, or stop decisions.

### Two short contrast examples

1. **Activity versus strategy**  
   “Post four times a week” versus a decision that explains audience, channel role, purpose, capacity, and review.
2. **Metric versus KPI**  
   A raw platform count versus a defined signal connected to an audience action and decision.

Examples must:

- be explicitly hypothetical;
- contain no invented client names;
- contain no revenue, ROAS, sales, reach, or growth outcome;
- avoid fabricated benchmark values;
- illustrate decisions rather than imply results.

## 19. Public-safe claim boundaries

The revision must preserve DaDaStore's practical, data-informed positioning.

Allowed:

- explain frameworks, prompts, and operating choices;
- recommend documenting evidence and assumptions;
- discuss tradeoffs and capacity;
- describe measurement and attribution limits;
- use hypothetical examples without outcomes;
- state that the template can improve decision clarity or coordination when used well, without guaranteeing it.

Disallowed:

- guaranteed growth, sales, reach, leads, engagement, or ROI;
- guaranteed return on ad spend;
- invented performance statistics;
- unsupported industry benchmarks;
- fake case studies or testimonials;
- private client names, messages, account data, or metrics;
- claims that one platform, cadence, pillar count, or KPI set fits every business;
- claims that social media alone caused a business outcome;
- legal, privacy, or compliance guarantees;
- presenting the template as proof of implementation quality.

## 20. Unsupported claims to remove or soften

The implementation unit must revise these current statements or implications:

- **“A strategy that ignores these limits usually creates an impressive calendar followed by inconsistent delivery.”**  
  Replace the predicted result with a conditional risk: ignoring capacity can make the plan difficult to sustain.
- **“Choose one priority audience for the cycle.”**  
  Present one primary audience as a focus option for lean teams, not a universal requirement.
- **“Create three to five pillars.”**  
  Present three to five only as an illustrative starting range where appropriate.
- **“A short explanation, a visual checklist, a comparison, and a question-led post can create enough variety.”**  
  Frame these as examples, not a sufficient format formula.
- **“Leaving response ownership undefined can damage an otherwise useful content system.”**  
  Use measured language: undefined ownership can create inconsistent handling or escalation risk.
- Any statement that treats platform diagnostics such as qualified reach or meaningful consumption as standardized without defining them.

No unsupported claim should be replaced with a different performance promise.
