How to Write a Creative Brief for Performance Marketing

Give creative teams useful context through audience evidence, objective, offer, message, format, constraints, hypotheses, and feedback rules.

By DaDaStoreEditorial draft8 min read

A performance creative brief should reduce ambiguity without dictating every frame. Weak briefs ask for more versions, provide a list of claims, and leave the team to guess the audience, evidence, placement, and decision. Strong briefs describe the problem to solve, the boundaries that matter, and the learning the work should create.

Structure the Brief

Run a brief-readiness checkpoint

Before production starts, ask a strategist, creator, media owner, and destination owner to explain the assignment in their own words. Their answers should agree on the audience situation, communication objective, proof boundary, offer, required formats, variable under test, and next decision. Differences reveal ambiguity while it is still inexpensive to correct.

The checkpoint should also confirm source files, product access, legal or policy review, landing-page dependencies, naming, due dates, and final approver. Record deliberate unknowns instead of filling them with assumptions. A brief can move forward with uncertainty when the test is designed to resolve it; it should stop when missing evidence would make the creative misleading or impossible to evaluate. That distinction protects both creative freedom and accountable performance work.

Keep the final brief concise enough to use during production while linking to deeper research and evidence. Creators should not have to search through a long strategy deck to find the actual assignment. Put the decision, audience, objective, proof, offer, constraints, variable, deliverables, and approver in the working view. Move supporting interviews, reports, and technical specifications into named references. This hierarchy preserves context without making the brief an archive of every conversation that preceded it.
